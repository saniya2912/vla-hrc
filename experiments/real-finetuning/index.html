Real-Data Fine-Tuning

Following the unsuccessful direct transfer of the simulation-trained policy, this experiment tests whether π0.5 can be fine-tuned directly from real-robot demonstrations instead — independently of the sim-to-real issues found on the previous page.

Overview

A simplified task, trained from scratch on real data

For this pilot, the task was simplified from full pick-and-place to pushing a single block to the far side of the workspace while keeping the gripper closed throughout, removing grasping and release from the learning problem. The model was initialised directly from the pretrained pi05_base checkpoint — no warm-start from a simulation-trained policy, and no simulation data of any kind.

Methodology

Real-data-only fine-tuning pilot

Demonstrations were collected using kinesthetic teaching rather than the scripted IK pipeline used elsewhere in this project: Baxter was placed in gravity-compensated, backdrivable mode, allowing the arm to be physically guided through the push motion while the raw joint trajectory was recorded, then resampled to 10 Hz and replayed under position control. The gripper was closed once at the start of each session and held closed throughout, with a fixed language prompt (“push the block to the far side”) to avoid colour-grounding ambiguity. Training used a batch size of 2, AdamW, and a cosine learning-rate schedule (peak 2×10-4, 500 warm-up steps, decay to 1×10-5 over 15,000 steps) — a shorter run than the simulation datasets, chosen to reduce the risk of overfitting the small real-robot dataset.

Results

Real-data fine-tuning performance

Demonstrations

28

Evaluation trials

25

Success rate

56%

The policy achieved 14 successful trials out of 25 (56%). The most common failure was pushing the block in the wrong direction (7 of 11 failures); three trials involved the arm hitting the table edge, and one block was knocked from the table. Measured displacement did not clearly separate successful and failed trials (mean 27.6 cm for successes vs. 23.6 cm for failures, with substantial overlap) — in one failed trial the block moved 17.4 cm, comparable to several successes, but towards the near side rather than the required far side, showing that displacement magnitude alone is not a reliable measure of task success. A further limitation: the 28 training demonstrations placed the block at almost the same image location (std. ≈2.6 cm), and evaluation trials were also close to this position (within ≈11.2 cm of the training centroid, most within 3.2 cm) — so the 56% figure is a measure of reliability on a scene close to the training distribution, not a measure of generalisation to new block placements.
